The Best Time to Visit Sedona? October Makes a Strong Case

Fall in Sedona means 70-degree afternoons, gold cottonwoods along Oak Creek, harvest in the Verde Valley vineyards and thinner crowds on the trails. Here's how to plan an October or November visit — hikes, wine, where to stay and what to book early.

By Jim Reich · October 3, 2026 · 4 min read

Ask anyone who lives here when you should come, and you’ll get the same slightly guilty answer: October. We’d love to tell you every month is equal — and we run tours every month — but if you can choose, choose fall. Here’s why, and how to make the most of it.

What fall actually feels like in Sedona

Sedona sits at about 4,350 feet, which is the whole trick. Phoenix is still touching 100°F in early October; up here the afternoons settle into the low-to-mid 70s and the mornings are crisp enough for a jacket on the first vineyard patio of the day. By November, highs drift into the 60s and nights get properly cold. Rain is rare. The light goes long and amber, which is what every photographer who’s ever pointed a camera at Cathedral Rock has been chasing.

The one thing to know: fall is not a secret anymore. October weekends are among the busiest of the year, and the two shuttle-only trailheads (Cathedral Rock and Soldier Pass) fill early. Midweek is calmer, and the first two weeks of November give you most of the color with noticeably fewer people.

Fall color: where and when

Sedona’s red rocks don’t change, obviously. The color comes from the trees along the water — cottonwoods, sycamores, velvet ash and bigtooth maples — so the show is concentrated in a few places:

  • Oak Creek Canyon (SR-89A north of town). The drive itself is the attraction; pull-offs at Midgley Bridge, Grasshopper Point and Slide Rock State Park give you the creek framed in gold. Peak color is usually the last week of October into the first ten days of November, later than most visitors expect.
  • West Fork Trail. The most famous fall hike in Arizona for good reason: a shaded canyon with a dozen creek crossings under maples that turn red and orange. The parking lot at Call of the Canyon fills by mid-morning on weekends; arrive at opening or go midweek.
  • Red Rock State Park and the Oak Creek stretch through Page Springs. Lower elevation, so the cottonwoods turn a week or two after the canyon. This is the same creek that runs past the vineyards — you can hike a loop at the park in the morning and taste at a winery on the same water in the afternoon.

Harvest in the Verde Valley

Here’s the part most Sedona fall guides leave out. The Verde Valley became an official American Viticultural Area in December 2021, and September through October is harvest. Depending on the week you visit, you’ll see bins of fruit coming in at Alcantara, Page Springs Cellars and Javelina Leap, smell fermentation in the tasting rooms, and — if you ask nicely and the winemaker is around — get a look at a barrel room in the middle of its busiest season.

It is also the season for the valley’s own festivals and release weekends, when the tasting rooms along the Page Springs corridor and in Old Town Cottonwood pour new vintages. We watch those calendars so you don’t have to; if your dates line up with a release or a festival, we’ll route the day through it.

A practical note: tasting rooms are busier in October, and a few of our favorite stops — DA Ranch, the Southwest Wine Center in Clarkdale, Bodega Pierce — have limited days or take reservations. That’s most of the reason a guided day works so well in fall: someone else holds the reservations and drives the switchbacks while you watch the color go by.

A three-day fall itinerary we’d actually book

Day one — arrive and get your bearings. Check in, then drive Oak Creek Canyon late afternoon when the light hits the west wall. Dinner in Uptown or West Sedona; most restaurants take reservations and fall weekends need them.

Day two — hike early, taste after. Be at a trailhead by 7:30. Bell Rock Pathway and Baldwin Trail are easy and spectacular; West Fork if you want the full color show. Afternoon: a wine tour. For a first visit we’d suggest four or five hours in the Page Springs vineyards; for a group that includes beer drinkers and non-drinkers, a Verde Valley loop that mixes a vineyard, a brewery and lunch in Old Town Cottonwood.

Day three — Jerome and back. The old copper-mining town sits a mile up Cleopatra Hill with views to the San Francisco Peaks, half a dozen tasting rooms, galleries and a famously good burger. Go in the morning before the tour buses, then wind back through Clarkdale and Cottonwood.

What to book early, and what to bring

Book early: lodging (October books out months ahead in Uptown and along Oak Creek), any Saturday wine tour, dinner reservations and the Cathedral Rock/Soldier Pass shuttle plan. Bring layers — a 45°F sunrise and a 75°F afternoon are the same day here — real walking shoes, sunglasses and a reusable water bottle. If you’re wine tasting, eat breakfast; the altitude makes a flight land harder than it does at sea level.

And if you can’t come in fall?

Spring (March through May) is the other peak, with wildflowers and the same mild temperatures, but windier and just as busy. Summer is hot by day — low 90s — yet cool by evening and quiet on the trails at dawn; the vineyards are green and lush. Winter is the sleeper: red rocks dusted with snow after a storm, empty trails, fireplaces in the tasting rooms and the best hotel rates of the year. We’ve had December tours that guests swore were their favorite day in Arizona.

But if the calendar is yours to choose: October. We’ll see you on the patio.
