Arizona Wine Guide for First-Timers: Grapes, Labels and How to Taste

A first-timer's Arizona wine guide from a Sedona guide since 2004: the grapes to know, how to read the label, tasting room basics and getting bottles home.

By Jim Reich · November 3, 2026 · 7 min read

Arizona wine is high-elevation wine, grown mostly between about 3,000 and 5,500 feet, and the state’s best bottles lean toward warm-climate grapes like Syrah, Grenache, Malvasia Bianca and Viognier rather than the usual Napa lineup. For a first-timer, this Arizona wine guide boils down to three things: know a handful of grapes, learn to read where the fruit was grown on the label, and start in the Verde Valley, where the most tasting rooms sit within half an hour of Sedona.

We have been pouring guests into Verde Valley tasting rooms since 2004, and the most common thing we hear at the end of the day is some version of “I had no idea.” Here is what we tell people before that first glass.

Arizona wine in 60 seconds

Arizona is a small wine state that is growing fast. The University of Arizona’s first statewide harvest report found about 532 acres harvested in 2025 and 1,187 tons of grapes, spread across nearly 80 varieties, slightly more red than white (University of Arizona). For perspective, that is a rounding error next to California, which is part of the fun: most Arizona wine is made in small lots and sold right in the tasting room.

Visitors have noticed. The Arizona Office of Tourism’s 2023 wine tourism survey estimated about $351 million in economic output from the industry, and found that visitor spending had grown 677% since 2011 (Arizona Office of Tourism).

The state has three federally recognized growing regions: Sonoita and Willcox in southeastern Arizona and the Verde Valley below Sedona. We compare them in detail in our guide to Arizona wine regions, so here we will stick to what a first-time taster needs to know.

Why altitude is the whole story

When people picture Arizona, they picture saguaros and 115-degree afternoons, and they assume grapes would bake. The vineyards are not down there. The Arizona Wine Growers Association describes vineyards planted roughly 3,000 to 5,500 feet above sea level, with most of the year’s rain arriving in the July and August monsoon (AWGA).

That elevation gives Arizona a big swing between hot days and cool nights. Warm days ripen the fruit; cool nights help it hold on to acidity, which is what keeps a wine fresh instead of flabby. When someone asks us “is Arizona wine good?”, that day-to-night swing is the short answer for why it can be. The longer answer is in the glass.

The Arizona wine grapes to know

You do not need to memorize 80 varieties. Learn these and you will recognize most of what is poured on a typical day.

Reds

  • Syrah. The most-planted grape in the state by acreage (University of Arizona). Expect it on its own and in blends.
  • Grenache. Number two statewide. Often blended with Syrah and Mourvèdre in the style of France’s Rhône Valley, so watch for “GSM” or Rhône-style blends on tasting menus.
  • Cabernet Sauvignon. Number three, for the guest who wants something familiar.
  • Italian and Spanish grapes. The Verde Valley in particular grows many minor and Italian varieties, including Barbera, Aglianico and Sangiovese (University of Arizona). The Verde Valley AVA also lists Petite Sirah and Tannat among its reds (Verde Valley AVA).

Whites

  • Malvasia Bianca. If Arizona has a signature white, this is a strong candidate. It is aromatic and floral, and it rounds out the state’s top six grapes along with Viognier and Chardonnay.
  • Viognier. A Rhône white and one of the state’s six most-planted grapes.
  • Chardonnay. The familiar one, and a good benchmark for comparing Arizona with wine regions you already know.
  • Vermentino and Picpoul Blanc. Both appear on the Verde Valley AVA’s list of featured whites, and both are worth trying on a warm patio (Verde Valley AVA).

Our advice to first-timers: taste at least one grape you have never heard of. That is where Arizona tends to surprise people.

How to read an Arizona wine label

This is the single most useful thing to learn, because not every bottle sold in an Arizona tasting room is made from Arizona fruit. Small wineries sometimes buy grapes from other states, especially in a short harvest year, and that is perfectly legal. The label tells you which is which.

Federal rules from the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau set the thresholds (TTB):

  • “Arizona” on the label: at least 75% of the wine comes from fruit grown in Arizona.
  • A county name, such as “Yavapai County”: at least 75% from that county.
  • An AVA, such as “Verde Valley,” “Willcox” or “Sonoita”: at least 85% from grapes grown in that viticultural area.
  • A broader appellation, such as “American” or another state’s name: the fruit was grown somewhere other than Arizona, or in a mix.

Since most of the state’s fruit comes from the Willcox AVA (University of Arizona), do not be surprised to see “Willcox” on a bottle poured in Cottonwood or Page Springs. It is still Arizona wine, just grown a few hours south. If you want to taste the place you are standing in, look for “Verde Valley” on the label, or simply ask the person pouring where the grapes were grown. Tasting room staff are usually happy to talk vineyards.

Your first Arizona wine tasting: how it works

If you have tasted in Napa or Paso Robles, you already know the drill. If not, here is what to expect.

  • Flights. Most tasting rooms offer a flight of several small pours for a set fee, often with a choice of whites, reds or a mix. Fees and formats vary; our guide to wine tasting in Sedona breaks down where to taste and what it typically costs.
  • Go light to dark. Start with whites and rosé, finish with the bigger reds. Your palate will thank you.
  • Use the dump bucket. Pouring out the rest of a taste is not rude. It is how you stay sharp through the fourth stop.
  • Ask questions. “Where were these grapes grown?” and “What are you excited about this year?” will get you further than any tasting note.
  • Pace yourself. Three or four stops makes a great day. Eat a real breakfast and plan lunch in the middle.
  • Wine clubs. Many wineries offer club memberships with shipments and member perks. Listen to the pitch if you love the wine, but there is no obligation to join.

Where first-timers should start

For most visitors, the answer is the Verde Valley, because you can see two very different styles of Arizona wine country in one day:

  • Page Springs and Cornville, where estate wineries sit among their own vines about 20 to 30 minutes from Sedona. See our Page Springs wine tasting guide.
  • Old Town Cottonwood, where you can walk between tasting rooms along Main Street. See our Old Town Cottonwood guide.
  • Jerome and Camp Verde, for a historic mining town or a quieter rural stop.

The Verde Valley Wine Consortium keeps an events calendar for the region, and our Sedona wineries and vineyards page lists the places we visit on tour.

Getting your Arizona wine home

You will probably buy a bottle or two. A few practical notes:

  • Flying. Under TSA rules, wine at 24% alcohol or less has no quantity limit in checked bags; check with your airline too (TSA). A full bottle will not make it through security in a carry-on because of the liquids limit, so pack it in your checked bag, wrapped in clothes or a wine sleeve.
  • Shipping. Many Arizona wineries can ship, but what they can send depends on your home state’s laws. Ask before you buy a case.
  • Driving. Keep bottles out of a hot trunk. A cooler bag in the back seat is cheap insurance.
