Grand Canyon in Winter 2026-27: What's Open, Snow, Roads and Tips

Visiting the Grand Canyon in winter 2026-27? A Sedona guide since 2004 covers what's open on the South Rim, water limits, fees, snowy roads and Hermit Road.

By Jim Reich · November 8, 2026 · 7 min read

Yes, you can visit the Grand Canyon in winter: the South Rim is open 24 hours a day, every day of the year, and a dusting of snow on the red and buff layers is one of the most beautiful things we have ever seen out there. This winter comes with some real asterisks, though, including South Rim water restrictions, reduced dining and a North Rim that closes for the season at the end of November. Here is what is open, what is not, and how we would plan a winter visit from Sedona, as of November 8, 2026.

We have been guiding in Sedona since 2004, and once the holidays get close, “is the Grand Canyon worth it in winter?” becomes one of the questions we hear most. Our short answer: yes, if you plan for cold, short days and a little less infrastructure than usual this year.

Is the Grand Canyon open in winter?

The South Rim is. The National Park Service keeps it open all year, and its viewpoints, the Rim Trail and Desert View Drive stay accessible through the season. Desert View Drive is open to all vehicles year-round “unless temporarily closed by snow,” according to the park’s operations update. The Visitor Center is open daily from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m., and the Park Store from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m.

The North Rim is a different story. It is open for day use only this fall, with no water, no lodging, no store and no gas. The paved Cape Royal and Point Imperial roads reopened on November 3 after repair work and stay open through November 30 or until the first major snowstorm, whichever comes first, per the NPS Things To Do page. After that, plan on the North Rim being closed until spring. From Sedona it is a four-hour drive each way anyway, so for a winter trip the South Rim is the only practical choice.

What is different at the South Rim this winter

This is the part to read carefully. Flooding on August 29 damaged the park’s water system, and a second flash flood on September 16 damaged the temporary intake, as ITPR reported. As of the park’s November 4 update:

  • Stage 4 water restrictions are in effect on the South Rim. Bring your own drinking water for the whole day and assume refill stations may not be available.
  • Overnight lodging inside the park (El Tovar, Bright Angel, Kachina, Thunderbird and Maswik) has been closed. Grand Canyon Lodges says the lodges are planning to reopen on November 20, and reservations from that date are proceeding as planned. Yavapai Lodge is listed as closed.
  • Dining is limited. El Tovar’s dining room and lounge are closed. Harvey House Cafe is serving breakfast and lunch, Arizona Steak House is open for dinner from 5 to 8:30 p.m., and Canyon Village Market is open 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. (deli closed). Hours are “subject to change,” so check before you count on a sit-down lunch.
  • Mather Campground is open year-round but is dry camping only, with no outdoor wood fires. Trailer Village and the laundry and showers are closed.
  • Phantom Ranch at the bottom of the canyon is closed through at least January 31, 2027, and overnight mule trips there are cancelled through February 2027.

The NPS has projected that pumping from a temporary intake could begin in mid-November, with tank refilling taking roughly 45 days after that, but those dates are tentative. Things should improve over the winter. Until they do, treat the South Rim as a day-use destination with snacks and water in the car.

Fees and passes for a winter visit

Winter does not change the entrance fee. Per the NPS fees page:

  • Private vehicle: $35, valid seven days, covering everyone in the car.
  • Per person (walking or cycling in): $20.
  • Grand Canyon annual pass: $70. The America the Beautiful annual pass is $80 for U.S. residents.
  • Nonresident fee: visitors who are not U.S. residents, age 16 and older, now pay an additional $100 per person unless they enter on an annual or America the Beautiful pass. The nonresident America the Beautiful pass is $250, which can make sense for a family visiting several parks.

The park accepts cards only, no cash, and no reservation or timed entry is needed. There are no free-entrance days on the NPS list between December and February.

Hermit Road, shuttles and the best winter viewpoints

Here is the winter bonus most people do not know about. From March 1 through November 30, Hermit Road on the west end of the Village is closed to private cars and served by the free Red Route shuttle. In December, January and February, when that bus is not running, private vehicles under 22 feet can drive it, according to the NPS. That means you can stop at Hopi Point, Mohave Point and Pima Point on your own schedule, which is a treat at sunset.

Other shuttle routes run on a reduced winter schedule; check the current shuttle times when you arrive, since they change with the season.

Our favorite winter stops:

  • Mather Point and Yavapai Point for the classic first look, a short walk from the Visitor Center.
  • Hopi Point on Hermit Road for sunset, with long views west and the river visible in places.
  • Desert View Watchtower, 25 miles east of the Village on Desert View Drive, for the stone tower and a view up toward the Painted Desert. Its shop and tower have posted hours that vary by season, so check the current hours before driving out late in the day.
  • The Rim Trail between the Village and Yavapai, which is paved and mostly level, but icy in shaded spots after a storm.

Mule rides along the rim are offered year-round on the South Rim, and the park’s bike rentals stay open when weather allows.

Snow, cold and driving from Sedona in winter

The South Rim sits around 7,000 feet, roughly 2,500 feet higher than Sedona, and it feels it. Expect cold mornings and nights well below freezing, daytime highs that are often only in the 30s and 40s, and occasional storms that can drop real snow. Sunset is around 5:15 p.m. in December, so daylight is short. Check the park’s weather and road conditions page and webcams the morning you go.

From Sedona, the South Rim is about 110 miles and two to two and a quarter hours in good conditions. In summer and fall we love the drive up Oak Creek Canyon on 89A to Flagstaff and out US-180. In winter we usually skip it. The switchbacks at the top of the canyon and the stretch of US-180 past the San Francisco Peaks are the first places to get slick. Our winter route is AZ-179 to I-17 north, I-40 west to Williams, then AZ-64 north. It is a bit longer, closer to two and a half hours, but it is mostly interstate and plowed early.

Before you leave, check AZ511 for chain controls and closures on I-17, I-40 and US-180. Fill up in Flagstaff, Williams or Tusayan, carry a blanket and water in the car, and give yourself enough time to be off the rim road before full dark. What to book, what to bring, when to go

What to book: if you want to stay at the rim after the planned November 20 reopening, book directly and keep an eye on the lodge alerts page. Otherwise, base yourself in Sedona, Flagstaff or Williams and do the canyon as a day trip. If you want a sit-down dinner at the rim, the Arizona Steak House is the main option this winter.

What to bring: a full day of drinking water for everyone (this year more than ever), snacks or a packed lunch, a warm insulated jacket, hat, gloves and boots with good tread. Traction devices for your shoes are worth their weight on the Rim Trail after a storm. Sunglasses, too: snow on the rim is bright.

When to go: weekdays in January and February are the quietest days of the year at the South Rim. Christmas week and Presidents’ Day weekend are busy. Arrive by mid-morning, after the overnight ice has softened, and plan to stay for sunset, then drive back to Sedona on the interstate route. If a storm is forecast, wait a day; the canyon after fresh snow is worth it.
