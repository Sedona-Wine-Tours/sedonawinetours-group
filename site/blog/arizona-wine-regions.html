Arizona Wine Regions: Verde Valley vs Willcox vs Sonoita, Explained

Arizona wine regions explained: how the Verde Valley, Willcox and Sonoita AVAs differ in elevation, climate and grapes, and which one fits your next trip.

By Jim Reich · October 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Arizona has three federally recognized wine regions: the Sonoita AVA south of Tucson, the Willcox AVA in the state’s southeastern corner, and the Verde Valley AVA just below Sedona. Willcox grows most of the state’s grapes, Sonoita is the oldest and most pastoral, and the Verde Valley has the most tasting rooms within easy reach of a hotel bed, which is why it is the Arizona wine region most visitors actually taste in.

We have been driving guests through Arizona wine country since 2004, back when “Arizona wine” got a laugh at the dinner table. It does not anymore. Visit Arizona notes that the number of wine producers in the state has grown about 200% since 2011 (Visit Arizona). Here is how the three Arizona wine regions differ, and how to choose between them.

What an AVA is, and why Arizona’s three matter

An American Viticultural Area, or AVA, is a grape-growing region recognized by the federal Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB) for its distinct climate, soils and geography. Once a region is an AVA, wineries can put its name on the label when the grapes meet the federal sourcing rules. The TTB’s list includes exactly three Arizona AVAs: Sonoita (Cochise, Pima and Santa Cruz counties), Willcox (Cochise and Graham counties) and Verde Valley (Yavapai County) (TTB).

The thing all three share is altitude. Arizona wine is not desert-floor wine. Vineyards sit roughly 3,000 to 5,500 feet above sea level, where hot days give way to cool nights, and most of the rain arrives in a burst during the July and August monsoon (Arizona Wine Growers Association). That day-to-night swing is what keeps acidity in the grapes, and it is the short answer to the question we hear on every tour: is Arizona wine good? Taste a few glasses and decide for yourself, but the growing conditions are real.

Sonoita: Arizona’s first wine region

Sonoita is where modern Arizona wine started. Visit Arizona credits a 1973 vineyard study by soil scientist Dr. Gordon Dutt with drawing winemakers to the grasslands southeast of Tucson, and Sonoita became the state’s first AVA in 1984 (Visit Arizona; Alcohol Professor).

  • Where: Around the small towns of Sonoita, Elgin and Patagonia, roughly 60 miles southeast of Tucson.
  • Elevation: About 4,500 to 5,200 feet.
  • Climate: Summer highs in the 80s and 90s, and 15 to 20 inches of rain a year, most of it during monsoon season (AWGA).
  • The feel: Rolling high grassland, oak-dotted hills and wide skies. Wineries are spread out along rural highways, so plan on driving between stops.

Sonoita is a wonderful weekend if you are already in Tucson. From Sedona it is a long haul, roughly four and a half hours each way, so we only suggest it for travelers building a full Arizona road trip.

Willcox: where most Arizona grapes are grown

If Sonoita is the pioneer, Willcox is the workhorse. Visit Arizona says about three-quarters of Arizona’s grapes come from the Willcox AVA (Visit Arizona).

The TTB made Willcox official effective October 12, 2016. The final rule describes about 526,000 acres in Graham and Cochise counties, and at the time of the petition there were 21 commercially producing vineyards covering about 454 acres, plus 18 wineries (Federal Register). A few details from that rule explain why growers like it:

  • A high basin ringed by mountains. The AVA sits below the surrounding ranges, so growing-season temperatures run warmer than in the mountains and storms often weaken before they arrive.
  • Cold air drainage. Cool air slides down from the mountains at night, which helps the grapes but raises spring frost risk. That is why you will see large frost fans in Willcox vineyards.
  • Big temperature swings. The Arizona Wine Growers Association notes daily swings of up to 50 degrees during the growing season, with vineyards planted between 4,000 and 5,500 feet (AWGA).

Willcox is about 80 miles east of Tucson on Interstate 10 and around five hours from Sedona. One practical takeaway for visitors who never make it that far: because so much of the state’s fruit grows there, do not be surprised to see “Willcox” on the label of a bottle poured in Cottonwood or Page Springs. Check the label to see where the fruit was grown.

Verde Valley AVA: Sedona’s wine country

The Verde Valley became Arizona’s third AVA in November 2021 (Wine Industry Advisor). It covers 219 square miles of northeastern Yavapai County, centered on the spot where Oak Creek meets the Verde River. At the time of the designation the region had 19 commercial vineyards farming more than 136 acres and 25 tasting rooms (Verde Valley AVA).

  • Where: The AWGA lists Sedona, Jerome, Camp Verde, Cottonwood and Clarkdale, plus the communities of Cornville, Page Springs and Rimrock (AWGA). Cottonwood, the hub, is about 100 miles north of Phoenix on I-17.
  • Elevation: Roughly 3,000 to 5,500 feet, the widest range of the three regions.
  • Rain: About 16 to 18 inches a year, again mostly in monsoon season.
  • Grapes: More than 40 varieties are grown here. Among whites the AVA highlights Malvasia Bianca, Viognier, Chardonnay, Vermentino, Seyval Blanc and Picpoul Blanc; among reds, Syrah, Petite Sirah, Cabernet Sauvignon, Sangiovese, Tannat and Barbera (Verde Valley AVA).

What makes the Verde Valley different for travelers is density. You can taste at creekside estate wineries in the morning and walk between a half-dozen tasting rooms in a historic downtown in the afternoon, all within about 30 minutes of Sedona. If you want the details, our guides to Page Springs wine tasting and Old Town Cottonwood wine tasting walk through each stop, and our Sedona wineries and vineyards page lists the places we visit.

It is also a teaching region. Yavapai College’s Southwest Wine Center, inside the AVA, runs viticulture and enology programs (Visit Arizona). Check the current hours before you go, since a college schedule does not always match a vacation schedule.

Verde Valley vs Willcox vs Sonoita: which Arizona wine region should you visit?

Here is how we would match each region to a trip:

  • You are staying in Sedona, Flagstaff or Phoenix: Verde Valley, without much debate. It has the most tasting rooms within a short drive, and you can pair wine with red rock scenery, Jerome and Old Town Cottonwood in a single day.
  • You are based in Tucson or southern Arizona: Sonoita is the easy day trip, and the scenery is a nice surprise for people who expect saguaros.
  • You want to see where Arizona’s grapes are grown at scale: Willcox, ideally as an overnight. Pair it with the rest of southeastern Arizona rather than treating it as a quick out-and-back.
  • You want to try all three: Start in the Verde Valley. With most of the state’s grapes grown in Willcox, labels in Verde Valley tasting rooms are a good way to sample the state’s range before committing to the long drive south.

When to go, what to book, what to bring

When to go. Fall is the sweet spot across all three regions. Harvest wraps up in late summer and early fall, afternoons are comfortable, and the light is beautiful. In the Verde Valley, October and November are our favorite months; our Sedona in November guide covers the weather. Spring is a close second. Summer works if you go early in the day, but July and August bring monsoon storms. Winter is quiet and pleasant, though some smaller wineries shorten their hours, so check before you drive.

What to book. Weekend afternoons fill up, and some wineries ask larger groups to reserve ahead, so check each winery’s policy. What to bring. Water, a light layer for the cooler late-afternoon air at elevation, sunscreen, a real meal before the first tasting, and a box or cooler bag if you plan to buy bottles. Several Verde Valley wineries welcome well-behaved dogs on their patios, but policies vary by property, so ask before you bring a four-legged guest.
