Sedona to Page AZ: Horseshoe Bend and Antelope Canyon Trip Guide

Planning Sedona to Page AZ this winter? A local guide's route, drive times, Horseshoe Bend and Antelope Canyon fees, holiday closures and when to stay over.

By Jim Reich · November 10, 2026 · 7 min read

Going from Sedona to Page, AZ is a long but rewarding drive: plan on roughly three and a half to four hours each way right now, because the Oak Creek Canyon stretch of 89A is closed and the route runs over SR 179 and I-17 to Flagstaff, then north on US 89. You can see Horseshoe Bend and Antelope Canyon in one very long day, but after two decades of sending guests up there, we think an overnight in Page makes the trip far more enjoyable, especially with short winter daylight.

Here is how we would plan it this winter, with the fees and rules verified against the official sources.

The route from Sedona to Page right now

Normally, many Sedona visitors would head north through Oak Creek Canyon to Flagstaff. After the November 1 flood, ADOT has kept SR 89A closed north of Slide Rock State Park with no reopening date, and is routing Sedona-to-Flagstaff traffic over SR 179 south to I-17, then north on I-17, according to the ADOT release carried by Signals AZ. Our Oak Creek Canyon closure post has the full rundown.

From Flagstaff, the drive is simple:

  1. Sedona to Flagstaff: SR 179 to I-17 north, then follow I-17 into Flagstaff and pick up US 89 north on the east side of town.
  2. Flagstaff to Cameron: US 89 drops off the pine country onto the high desert of the Navajo Nation. Cameron is a natural stop for fuel, restrooms and a stretch.
  3. Cameron to Page: Keep north on US 89 past the Echo Cliffs and up to Page. Horseshoe Bend sits on the south edge of town, just off US 89, so it is often the first stop.

Drive times are estimates for good conditions. Northern Arizona highways can see snow and ice between November and March, so check AZ511 the morning you leave and leave room in the schedule. Fuel up in Flagstaff or Cameron; services are spread out on US 89.

Horseshoe Bend: fees, trail and tips

Horseshoe Bend is the famous overlook where the Colorado River loops around a sandstone point. The overlook is inside Glen Canyon National Recreation Area, but the parking lot belongs to the City of Page, which is why the fee works differently than you might expect.

  • Parking fee: The City of Page 2026 fee schedule lists $10 for a passenger vehicle (including RVs) and $5 for a motorcycle.
  • Park passes do not cover it: The National Park Service says America the Beautiful and other NPS passes do not apply to the city parking lot.
  • The walk: NPS describes the hike to the overlook as 1.5 miles round trip on a hardened path. There are two shade structures along the trail, none at the overlook, and no water or restrooms on the trail itself, so use the restrooms at the lot first.
  • Edges: Railings are only at the main overlook platform. Keep a close eye on kids, and give yourself room near the rim for photos.
  • Leashed dogs are allowed, per the NPS page, which is handy if your pup is on the road trip with you.
  • Drones are prohibited without the superintendent’s approval.

In winter, the hike is pleasant at midday, and the cool air is a real advantage over summer, when NPS warns about desert heat. Late afternoon light is beautiful, but sunset comes early this time of year, so plan the hike back to the car.

Antelope Canyon: guides, fees and holiday closures

Antelope Canyon is on Navajo Nation land, inside the Lake Powell Navajo Tribal Park, and the rules are firm. According to Navajo Nation Parks & Recreation:

  • A guide is mandatory. You cannot walk into Upper or Lower Antelope Canyon on your own. Visits are by guided tour only, booked through an authorized Navajo-guided operator.
  • There is a tribal park entry fee of $15 per person, per entry, on top of the guided tour price.
  • Upper Antelope Canyon has a two-hour visit limit.
  • Holiday closures: the park lists closures on Thanksgiving Day, Navajo Nation Family Day (the day after Thanksgiving), Christmas Day and New Year’s Day. If your trip lands on Thanksgiving weekend, that takes out both Thursday and Friday.

Our practical advice: book your Antelope Canyon time slot before you book anything else, then build the rest of the itinerary around it. Slots fill early on weekends and around the holidays, and midday slots tend to go first because that is when light reaches deeper into the slots. Bring a phone or camera you can operate quickly, since tours keep moving.

A quick time zone note: Arizona does not observe daylight saving time, but the Navajo Nation does. Between November and March, both are on Mountain Standard Time, so your tour time and your phone will agree. In summer, double-check which time your confirmation uses.

Other stops worth your time in Page

If you stay the night, Page rewards a slower pace.

  • Glen Canyon Dam and the Carl Hayden Visitor Center. The visitor center sits right at the dam and is a good place to understand how Lake Powell came to be. The NPS visitor centers page has current hours, and the park’s seasonal hours page notes that many services are reduced for the season, so check the current hours before you go or call the park at (928) 608-6200.
  • Lake Powell viewpoints. Glen Canyon is open 24 hours a day year round, per NPS. Areas like Wahweap require the park entrance fee, which is $30 per private vehicle for seven days according to the Glen Canyon fees page. Entrance stations take cards only, not cash. Note that this is separate from the Horseshoe Bend parking fee.
  • Sunset Crater and Wupatki on the way home. The loop road off US 89 just north of Flagstaff passes cinder cones and ancestral Puebloan pueblos. It is a separate NPS fee area; check nps.gov/sucr for current hours and fees before you add it.

If you want to string the Grand Canyon onto this trip, SR 64 from Cameron leads west toward the Desert View area of the South Rim. Our Grand Canyon in winter guide covers what is open this season, and our Sedona to Grand Canyon day trip post has the timing if you would rather do the canyon as its own day.

Day trip or overnight?

We get this question constantly, and our answer depends on the season.

A day trip is possible if you start early, already have an Antelope Canyon slot, and are comfortable with seven or more hours of driving round trip. In winter, that means leaving Sedona before sunrise and driving home in the dark, often on cold, possibly icy roads around Flagstaff.

An overnight is better for most people. Drive up in the afternoon, catch Horseshoe Bend in late light, sleep in Page, take a morning or midday Antelope Canyon tour, visit the dam, and head home with daylight to spare. You see more, you drive tired less, and you have a buffer if weather slows you down.

Three-day loop: Sedona to Page, then Page to the Grand Canyon South Rim via Cameron and SR 64, then back to Sedona. This is the classic northern Arizona circuit, and winter brings thinner crowds at every stop.

What to book, what to bring, when to go

Book: your Antelope Canyon tour first (with an authorized Navajo-guided operator), then lodging in Page, then dinner. Confirm any Sedona lodging too, since a few creekside properties are still recovering from the flood.

Bring:

  • Payment for the $10 Horseshoe Bend parking fee, plus a card for any NPS entrance fee (Glen Canyon stations do not take cash)
  • Water and snacks for the car; services are thin between Cameron and Page
  • Layers, a warm hat and gloves; mornings in Page and Flagstaff are cold in winter
  • Sturdy shoes with grip for sand and slickrock
  • A charged phone with offline maps, since cell coverage is spotty on stretches of US 89

When to go: November through February brings the quietest trails and the coolest walking weather, with the trade-off of short days and possible snow around Flagstaff. Avoid Thanksgiving Day, the day after, Christmas Day and New Year’s Day if Antelope Canyon is the main event. March and April bring longer days, and fall is our favorite overall.

Make room for a wine day before or after

A Page trip is a big driving day, so many of our guests bookend it with something easy closer to Sedona. A day in Verde Valley wine country, with the tasting rooms in Page Springs, Cornville, Old Town Cottonwood and Jerome, is all canyon-free driving and pairs well with a long road trip because someone else can do the driving.
