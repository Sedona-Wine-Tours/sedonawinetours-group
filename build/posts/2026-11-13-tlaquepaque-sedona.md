Tlaquepaque Sedona is worth the stop: a free-to-enter arts village on State Route 179 with more than 50 shops, galleries and places to eat, set in cobblestone courtyards under old sycamores along Oak Creek. Plan on one to three hours, come in the morning or late afternoon for easier parking, and know that the village is open 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. Monday through Thursday and 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Friday through Sunday, with restaurants staying open later.

We have been guiding visitors around Sedona since 2004, and Tlaquepaque (say "tla-keh-PAH-keh") is the place we most often suggest for guests who want a slower half-day off the trails. It also pairs well with an afternoon in wine country, which we will get to below. Everything here is current as of mid-November 2026 from the village's own site and the local tourism bureau; hours change, so check the current hours before you go.

## What Tlaquepaque Sedona is (and why it feels different)

Tlaquepaque Arts & Shopping Village was built in the 1970s by Nevada businessman Abe Miller, who, according to the [village's history page](https://tlaq.com/history), found a grove of century-old sycamores along Oak Creek and designed the village around them rather than clearing them. Architect Bob McIntyre modeled the buildings on San Pedro Tlaquepaque, a historic arts town near Guadalajara, Mexico. The village says the name means "the best of everything."

What that means on the ground: stucco walls, wrought-iron balconies, tiled fountains, bell towers and a series of courtyards that open into one another. It is one of the few places in Sedona where the shade and the architecture are the attraction as much as the shopping. Even guests who do not plan to buy anything tend to spend an hour just walking it.

There are two parts:

- **Tlaquepaque South** (336 State Route 179) is the original village, the one in most photos, with the chapel, the main courtyards and most of the galleries.
- **Tlaquepaque North** (313 State Route 179) sits across the highway and is newer, with more shops and restaurants and the lot where the holiday tree goes up.

Both are listed on the [Tlaquepaque hours page](https://tlaq.com/hours), which counts "over 50 world-class shops, galleries, and dining experiences" between them.

## Tlaquepaque hours and holiday closures

Per the [village's hours page](https://tlaq.com/hours):

- **Monday through Thursday:** 10 a.m. to 5 p.m.
- **Friday through Sunday:** 10 a.m. to 6 p.m.
- **Restaurants:** "extended evening hours," so dinner is an option after the shops close.
- **Closed:** Thanksgiving Day and Christmas Day. This year that means Thursday, November 26, and Friday, December 25.

Individual galleries and shops set their own schedules within those hours, so if there is one artist you are making the trip for, call ahead. The village's main line is (928) 282-4838.

## Tlaquepaque parking and getting there

The [Tlaquepaque parking page](https://tlaq.com/parking) says parking is free for village visitors, with several lots around Tlaquepaque South and a dedicated lot across the street from the North entrance. Larger vehicles and oversized rigs are directed to the Portal Lane lot northwest of the main village. The hours page describes onsite parking as "limited," which matches what we see: on a fall or spring Saturday between about 11 a.m. and 3 p.m., the lots fill and you may circle.

A few practical tips from years of dropping guests here:

- **Arrive before 11 a.m. or after 3 p.m.** on weekends and holidays.
- **Turning in:** Tlaquepaque sits just south of the roundabout where 179 meets 89A in Uptown. Traffic on 179 backs up on busy afternoons, so give yourself extra time.
- **Walk from Uptown.** If you are already parked in Uptown, Tlaquepaque is a short walk south past the roundabout.
- **Skip the car.** The regional Verde Shuttle bus lists a Tlaquepaque stop on its route between Cottonwood and Uptown; our [Sedona without a car guide](/blog/sedona-without-a-car) covers schedules and caveats.
- **Accessibility.** The village says accessible parking, entrances and restrooms are available throughout North and South. Wheelchairs are not available to rent on site, so bring your own mobility device. Cobblestones and a few stairways make some corners slower going.

## Where to eat at Tlaquepaque

The [village's food page](https://tlaq.com/foods/) lists a solid mix of sit-down meals and quick treats:

- **René at Tlaquepaque.** French-Mediterranean fine dining with a patio. This is the special-occasion table in the village.
- **El Rincon Mexican Restaurante.** Southwestern Mexican food with native influences, and a long-running local favorite.
- **Oak Creek Brewery & Grill.** American fare with house-brewed beer, upstairs at Tlaquepaque South. We covered it in our [Sedona breweries guide](/blog/sedona-breweries).
- **Secret Garden Cafe.** Breakfast, lunch and dinner in a garden courtyard.
- **Pump House Station & Urban Eatery.** Breakfast, lunch, dinner and drinks at Tlaquepaque North.
- **Sweets and coffee:** Cake Couture Coffee & Dessert, Cream Handmade Ice Cream, Dulce (ice cream, churros and candy) and The Chai Spot.

Our pattern for guests: a late breakfast or lunch at Secret Garden or El Rincon, an hour or two in the galleries, and then out to the vineyards for the afternoon. If you would rather end the day here, René and El Rincon both make an easy dinner after a wine tour.

One thing to know: Tlaquepaque does not have a winery tasting room of its own. For Arizona wine poured by the people who grow it, you head out of town to Page Springs, Cornville or Old Town Cottonwood, which is exactly why it pairs so well with a wine day.

## The Tlaquepaque chapel, galleries and what to see

**The chapel.** The small Tlaquepaque Chapel, tucked into the South village, is the most photographed corner of the property and a popular spot for small weddings. Its Spanish Colonial and Mexican details fit the rest of the village, and according to the [village's about page](https://tlaq.com/about), Sedona's Historic Preservation Commission designated it a local historic landmark in May 2024. Step in quietly; it is sometimes reserved for ceremonies.

**The galleries.** Sedona has a deep art scene, and Tlaquepaque concentrates a good share of it: painting, sculpture, glass, jewelry, Native American and Southwestern work, and fine crafts. Prices range from small gifts to serious collector pieces. Gallery staff here are used to browsers, so do not be shy about asking about an artist.

**The sycamores and the creek side.** The trees the village was built around are still there, and in fall they turn gold, which makes November one of the better months of the year to visit. Walk the edges of the South village where it backs toward Oak Creek for the quietest corners.

**The bell tower and fountains.** Kids and photographers both gravitate to the fountains in the central courtyards; the bell tower is the best landmark if your group splits up.

## Tlaquepaque at the holidays

The village is at its best in December.

- **Sedona Tree Lighting and Santa Visit, Friday, December 4, 2026,** 5 to 6:30 p.m. at Tlaquepaque North. Admission is free, and Visit Sedona lists a 26-foot tree, Santa's arrival, children's activities and complimentary hot chocolate ([Visit Sedona](https://visitsedona.com/events-calendar/tlaquepaque-christmas-tree-lighting/)). Arrive by 4:30 p.m. for parking.
- **Festival of Lights.** Sedona's best-loved holiday tradition. At the 5 p.m. tolling of the bell tower, thousands of luminarias are lit through the courtyards and walkways; Visit Sedona's most recent listing describes 6,000 luminarias, free admission and a 3 to 8 p.m. window ([Visit Sedona](https://visitsedona.com/events-calendar/festival-of-lights)). It is traditionally held in mid-December, but the 2026 date had not been posted when we wrote this, so confirm it with the village before you plan around it.

## When to go, what to bring and what to book

**When to go:** Weekday mornings are the calmest. Late afternoon works well in fall and winter, when the light is soft and you can roll straight into dinner. Mid-November through early December is a sweet spot: fall color in the sycamores, holiday decorations going up and smaller crowds than spring break or the Festival of Lights.

**What to bring:** Comfortable shoes for cobblestones and stairs, a light jacket (the courtyards stay shady and cool on winter afternoons), and room in your suitcase if you are a gallery person. Many galleries ship, so ask before you try to carry a sculpture home.

**What to book:** Dinner at René on a weekend or holiday, and any wine tour you want to pair with the visit. Tlaquepaque itself needs no tickets.

**How to make it a full day:** Spend the morning at Tlaquepaque, then head out to the vineyards. Page Springs Road in Cornville is roughly 20 to 30 minutes away (our [Page Springs wine tasting guide](/blog/page-springs-wine-tasting) covers the wineries), and the full list is on our [Sedona wineries and vineyards page](/sedona-wineries-and-vineyards). For more Arizona ideas, [Visit Arizona's Tlaquepaque listing](https://www.visitarizona.com/directory/tlaquepaque-arts-shopping-village/) is a handy starting point.
