Sedona stargazing is some of the best you can do without leaving a town with hotels, restaurants and a wine list. Sedona has been an International Dark Sky Community since 2014, so on a clear, moonless fall night you can stand at a trailhead ten minutes from Uptown and see the Milky Way arch over the red rocks. The trick is picking the right night and the right pullout, and this guide covers both.

We've been driving these roads after dark since 2004, mostly bringing guests home from Verde Valley tasting rooms, and the number of people who stop mid-sentence when they step out of the van and look up never gets old. Here's how to plan it on purpose.

## Why Sedona Is a Dark Sky Destination

In 2014, the International Dark-Sky Association (now [DarkSky International](https://darksky.org/sedona)) named Sedona the eighth International Dark Sky Community in the world. The Village of Oak Creek, just south of town, earned the same designation in 2016, according to the [Sedona Chamber of Commerce](https://sedonachamber.com/2023/10/05/are-you-an-astrophile/). That means shielded, downward-pointing outdoor lighting, warmer bulbs and a city lighting code that local nonprofit Keep Sedona Beautiful has spent years helping residents and businesses meet.

Up the hill, Flagstaff became the world's first International Dark Sky City on October 24, 2001, per [Discover Flagstaff](https://flagstaffarizona.org/things-to-do/astrotourism/). That makes this October the 25th anniversary of the designation, a nice excuse to fold a Flagstaff observatory night into a northern Arizona trip.

What you get in practice: dry high-desert air, an elevation of roughly 4,500 feet in town, and very little sky glow once you're a few minutes from the main roads. Fall is our favorite season for it. Monsoon clouds are gone, nights are long, and it gets dark early enough that you don't have to stay up until midnight.

## Best Places to Stargaze in Sedona

The Sedona Chamber's [Secret 7 stargazing list](https://visitsedona.com/sedona-secret-7/stargazing/) is the best starting point, and it lines up with where we'd send friends:

- **Beaverhead Flat Scenic Overlook** - A seldom-used pullout off State Route 179 south of the Village of Oak Creek. This is the one the Chamber recommends for the Milky Way, and it's our pick for the darkest sky with the least effort.
- **Baldwin Trailhead** - Near Red Rock Crossing, where the stars frame the silhouette of Cathedral Rock. Go here for the photograph.
- **Centennial Trailhead** - A short paved path off Airport Road with views of both the valley and the sky. The easiest option for anyone with mobility concerns.
- **Brins Mesa Trailhead** - Close to Uptown but tucked enough into the canyon to block the glow.
- **Thunder Mountain Trailhead** - West Sedona, with a wide open, nearly 360-degree view.
- **Fay Canyon Trailhead** - Out on Boynton Pass Road, quiet and dark, and the Chamber's suggestion for shooting stars.
- **Aerie Trailhead** - Also off Boynton Pass Road, with big sky and red rock walls on the horizon.

A few practical notes. Stick to trailheads and established pullouts rather than wandering onto trails in the dark, and never climb on formations at night. Many of these are Coconino National Forest sites, and some of the trailhead lots require a Red Rock Pass (or an America the Beautiful interagency pass) to park. Check the current rules and fees on the [Red Rock Pass Q&A](https://visitsedona.com/trip-planning/red-rock-pass-program-qa/) before you go. The Sedona Visitor Information Center at 331 Forest Road (928-282-7890) can tell you about current parking and access.

If you're traveling with a dog, the calm, quiet trailheads are good company for them too. Our [dog-friendly Sedona guide](/blog/dog-friendly-sedona) covers leash rules and pet-friendly patios for the rest of the day.

## Fall 2026 Dark Sky Calendar: New Moons and Meteor Showers

The moon matters more than anything else. A full moon in Sedona is beautiful on the rocks but washes out all but the brightest stars. Plan around these dates:

- **Orionid meteor shower, night of October 21-22** - A waxing gibbous moon is up in the evening, so [Space.com](https://www.space.com/orionid-meteor-shower-2026-guide-when-to-see-it) recommends the hours between midnight and dawn, after moonset. Expect around 10 to 20 meteors per hour under ideal skies.
- **Full moon, October 26** - Great for a moonlit red rock evening, not for stars.
- **New moon, around November 9** - The darkest stretch of the fall. The week on either side is prime Milky Way and deep-sky time, though by November the core of the Milky Way sets early in the evening, so look west right after dark.
- **Leonid meteor shower, night of November 17-18** - A half-lit moon sets around midnight, which leaves the best pre-dawn window dark. Rates are modest in a normal year.
- **Full moon, November 24** - Thanksgiving week is bright. Plan a sunset dinner instead.
- **Geminid meteor shower, night of December 13-14** - The big one. The [Farmers' Almanac](https://www.farmersalmanac.com/the-december-geminids-19860) notes a thin crescent moon setting around 9 PM, with 75 to 150 meteors per hour possible under dark skies. Sedona in mid-December gets cold after dark, but this is the night to bundle up.

Timing in Arizona is simple because we don't change the clocks. In mid-October the sun sets around 5:50 PM and real darkness arrives a little over an hour later. By mid-November sunset is closer to 5:20 PM, with a fully dark sky by about 6:45 PM. That's early enough to stargaze before a late dinner.

## Lowell Observatory: Telescopes in Flagstaff

If you want to look through a real telescope, [Lowell Observatory](https://lowell.edu/visit/) in Flagstaff is about an hour's drive north of Sedona through Oak Creek Canyon. This is where Pluto was discovered in 1930, and on clear nights visitors can view through the historic Clark Telescope and the six telescopes of the Giovale Open Deck Observatory.

Lowell's posted hours are noon to 10 PM Monday and Wednesday through Sunday, closed Tuesdays, plus closures on Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Eve, Christmas Day and New Year's Day. General admission doesn't require a reservation, but premium experiences do. Check the current hours and ticket prices on Lowell's site before you drive up, since telescope viewing depends on weather and schedules change.

Two tips if you go. First, Flagstaff sits near 7,000 feet, so it's noticeably colder than Sedona; bring a real jacket. Second, the drive back down 89A through the canyon is narrow and winding at night. Take it slowly, or have someone else drive. If you're making a full northern Arizona trip of it, our post on [Flagstaff fall colors](/blog/flagstaff-fall-colors) pairs well with an afternoon there before the observatory opens its evening programs.

## What to Bring and When to Go

**When to go:** Pick a night within about a week of the new moon, check the forecast for clear skies, and arrive 30 to 45 minutes after sunset. Give your eyes 20 minutes to adjust before you decide what you can see.

**What to bring:**

- Warm layers. Fall nights in Sedona cool off quickly once the sun goes down, and December nights can drop below freezing.
- A red flashlight or red-light mode on your headlamp. White light ruins night vision for you and everyone around you.
- Camp chairs or a blanket. Lying back is the most comfortable way to watch for meteors.
- Water and a snack, and a bag to pack out everything you bring in.
- A stargazing app for identifying constellations, set to night mode.
- For photos: a tripod and a camera or phone with a night or long-exposure mode.

**What to book:** Have dinner reservations sorted before dark, especially on weekends. Our list of [Sedona restaurants with a view](/blog/sedona-restaurants-with-a-view) is a good place to start for a sunset meal before you head out.

## Stargazing After a Day in Wine Country

Here's how a lot of our guests end up doing it. Spend the afternoon tasting in Page Springs, Cornville or Old Town Cottonwood, get back to Sedona around sunset, have a relaxed dinner, then drive out to Beaverhead Flat or Baldwin Trailhead once it's fully dark. Nobody in the group has to drive between tasting rooms, and by the time the stars are out everyone has had dinner and a few hours to reset. (If you've been tasting, let someone who hasn't take the wheel for the night drive.)
